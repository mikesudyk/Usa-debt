1790

Hamilton’s Funding Plan

The new federal government assumes Revolutionary War debts of the states and Continental Congress. Creditworthiness becomes a national priority.

After the Revolution the country owed a lot of money. Alexander Hamilton said the new government should pay all those old debts so people would trust America.
The Funding Act of 1790 and Assumption Act consolidated state and national war debts into federal obligations. Hamilton argued that a funded national debt would bind creditors to the new government and establish public credit. Result: the U.S. began with a deliberate policy of honoring its debts in full.
Key issue: Building trust so the young nation could borrow in the future. Result: Federal debt becomes a tool of nation-building, not just a wartime necessity.
1792

Coinage Act — Gold & Silver Standard

Congress defines the dollar in terms of gold and silver. Money is physical metal; government borrowing is expected to be repaid in hard currency.

Money was shiny coins made of real gold and silver. If the government needed more money, it couldn’t just print paper — it had to get more metal.
The Coinage Act of 1792 established a bimetallic standard (15:1 silver-to-gold ratio). This hard-money rule constrained both inflation and easy peacetime spending. Debt from the Revolution was real and expected to be serviced in specie. Philosophy: sound money as a limit on government.
Result: Long periods of price stability. Debt existed but remained modest outside of wars. The dollar’s value was tied to metal, not political discretion.
1812–1815

War of 1812

Another war forces heavy borrowing and temporary suspension of specie payments. Debt rises, then is gradually reduced afterward.

America fought another war. The government borrowed money to pay for it, and for a while people couldn’t always trade their paper for gold or silver coins.
The War of 1812 roughly tripled the federal debt. Banks suspended specie payments. After the war the Second Bank of the United States was chartered (1816) and efforts were made to restore convertibility and reduce debt. Pattern: war spikes debt; peacetime under hard money tends to bring it back down.
Pattern: Wars temporarily override the hard-money constraint. Restoring it afterward requires political will and economic surplus.
1835

Debt Nearly Paid Off

Under Andrew Jackson the federal debt falls to almost zero — the only time in U.S. history.

The government paid back almost all the money it owed. For a short time America had basically no national debt.
Jackson’s administration used surpluses (largely from land sales and tariffs) to retire the debt. This remains the sole period of near-zero federal debt. It demonstrated that sustained surpluses were politically and economically possible under the hard-money regime. Jackson also opposed the Second Bank, reflecting ongoing tension over how much financial power the federal government should have.
Unique moment: Only time the U.S. has been essentially debt-free. Shows the hard-money system could produce both borrowing capacity and repayment.
1830s–1850s

Hard Money Continues

Between the near-zero debt and the Civil War, the federal government largely stays within the specie standard. Debt remains modest.

For many years after that, the government mostly used real gold and silver money and didn’t borrow huge amounts.
Tariff revenue and land sales funded most peacetime needs. The Independent Treasury system (1840s) kept federal funds out of private banks. Prices were relatively stable over long periods. The system’s constraint was real: large new spending required either taxes, land sales, or borrowing that markets would accept under a specie standard.
Era takeaway: Hard money + limited peacetime government kept debt low and prices stable for decades — until the Civil War broke the pattern.
1861–1879

Civil War Greenbacks & Return to Gold

War forces unbacked paper money. Debt explodes. After the war the country works for years to restore gold convertibility.

During the big war the government printed special paper money that wasn’t backed by gold so it could pay soldiers. Prices went up. After the war people argued for years until the government promised again that paper money could be traded for gold.
Debt rose from ~$65 million (1860) to $2.7 billion (1866). The Legal Tender Acts (1862–63) created greenbacks — fiat notes not redeemable in specie. Inflation followed. After the war, the Resumption Act of 1875 set a date (1 January 1879) to restore gold convertibility. The effort succeeded, but only after a long political fight between “hard-money” and “soft-money” factions. Key figures: Salmon P. Chase (Treasury), later debates involving John Sherman and the gold-standard advocates versus greenback supporters.
Debt path: $65M → $2.7B. Philosophy: War temporarily overrides the hard-money constraint; restoring it requires political will and surplus. Result: Multi-decade effort to return to gold and reduce debt.
1913

Federal Reserve Act

A central bank is created that can expand credit and act as lender of last resort, even while gold convertibility still exists.

A new big bank was made that could help create more money when needed and lend to other banks if they got in trouble.
The Federal Reserve Act (1913), signed by Woodrow Wilson, created a central bank with regional reserve banks and a Board. Key architects included Carter Glass and H. Parker Willis; Nelson Aldrich had earlier proposed a more banker-controlled version. The Fed could expand or contract credit and serve as lender of last resort. Gold convertibility still existed, but the system now contained discretionary management — a philosophical step away from pure automatic convertibility toward active monetary policy.
Key legislation: Federal Reserve Act. Philosophy: Elastic currency and crisis management inside a still-gold-linked system. Result: Government gains a tool to influence money and credit.
1917–1919

World War I Financing

Massive Liberty Bond drives and tax increases fund the war. Debt rises sharply; the Fed supports the effort.

The government asked people to buy special war bonds to help pay for the fight. It also raised taxes. The amount the country owed grew a lot.
WWI was financed through a mix of taxation (including the new income tax) and large Liberty Bond issues. The Fed helped keep rates low to support bond sales. Debt rose from under $1 billion to over $25 billion. After the war, efforts were made to reduce debt, but the experience showed that modern total war required large-scale borrowing and central-bank cooperation — further eroding the pure hard-money ideal.
Key people: William Gibbs McAdoo (Treasury). Result: Debt jumps; postwar attempts to return toward prewar norms are only partly successful.
1933–1934

End of Domestic Gold Standard

FDR requires Americans to turn in gold. Dollar is devalued. Private gold ownership ends. Domestic dollar becomes fiat.

The president said people had to give their gold coins to the government. Paper money was no longer tied to gold for regular people. This gave the government more power over money.
Executive Order 6102 (1933) and the Gold Reserve Act (1934) ended domestic convertibility and devalued the dollar ~40% against gold (from $20.67 to $35/oz). Motivation: combat Depression deflation and give the government more room to expand the money supply and spend. Key figures: Franklin D. Roosevelt, Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau. Result: Americans could no longer redeem dollars for gold; an international gold link remained for foreign governments. This was a major philosophical shift toward managed money.
Key legislation: Gold Reserve Act. Philosophy: Active management to fight deflation. Result: Domestic dollar becomes fiat; government gains greater control over the money supply.
1944

Bretton Woods

Dollar becomes the world’s anchor currency, convertible to gold for foreign governments at $35/oz. Other currencies peg to the dollar.

After the big war, other countries agreed to use the American dollar as their main money, and America promised they could trade dollars for gold.
The Bretton Woods Agreement (1944) created a gold-dollar standard internationally while Americans already used fiat domestically. Foreign governments could redeem dollars for gold at $35/oz; other currencies pegged to the dollar. Key architects: John Maynard Keynes (UK) and Harry Dexter White (US). The system reflected a philosophy of managed international monetary order rather than pure free-market gold flows. Post-WWII debt/GDP peaked near 113% then fell sharply during the boom as the economy grew faster than debt.
Key people: Keynes, Harry Dexter White. Philosophy: Dollar as the world’s reserve asset, with a residual gold link for governments. Result: U.S. gains seigniorage and policy flexibility, but faces the later “Triffin dilemma.”
15 August 1971

Nixon Closes the Gold Window

The last formal link between the dollar and gold is severed. Pure fiat money begins. Currencies soon float.

The president said other countries could no longer trade their dollars for American gold. After that, money was just paper and computer numbers, and the government could create more of it more easily.
The “Nixon Shock” ended Bretton Woods convertibility. Spending pressures from the Vietnam War and Great Society programs had strained the $35/oz gold promise (the Triffin dilemma). Key figures: President Richard Nixon, Treasury Secretary John Connally, Fed Chair Arthur Burns. Result: fully managed fiat dollar, floating exchange rates by 1973, and complete Fed discretion over the money supply. Mild positive inflation later becomes explicit policy.
Philosophical shift: From commodity or externally constrained money to pure fiat. Key people: Nixon, Connally, Burns. Result: Governments gain full control over the domestic money supply.
1970s–1980s

Inflation, Volcker Shock, and the Rise of Deficits

High inflation is tamed by tight money. Peacetime deficits become structural as spending grows and tax cuts are hard to reverse.

Prices went up a lot in the 1970s. Then a new Fed chairman made borrowing more expensive so prices would stop rising so fast. At the same time the government kept borrowing even without a war.
The 1970s saw double-digit inflation. Fed Chair Paul Volcker (appointed 1979) raised rates sharply in the early 1980s, inducing a recession that broke the inflation spiral. Under Reagan, large tax cuts (Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981) combined with defense spending produced persistent peacetime deficits. Debt crossed $1 trillion in the early 1980s. Philosophy: monetary restraint can control inflation, but fiscal politics favor deficits.
Key people: Paul Volcker, Ronald Reagan. Legislation: ERTA 1981. Result: Inflation is conquered; structural peacetime deficits become normal.
1990s

Brief Surpluses, Then Return to Deficits

Strong growth and spending restraint produce brief surpluses. Debt/GDP falls, then deficits resume.

For a little while in the 1990s the government took in more money than it spent. Then it started borrowing again.
The late 1990s saw budget surpluses under Clinton, aided by the tech boom, spending restraint (including the 1990 and 1993 budget deals), and capital gains revenue. Debt held by the public fell as a share of GDP. After 2001, tax cuts, recession, and rising spending ended the surpluses. Key figures: Bill Clinton, Fed Chair Alan Greenspan. Philosophy: growth can reduce debt ratios, but political incentives make surpluses hard to sustain.
Result: Debt/GDP declines temporarily, then reverses. Shows that fiscal improvement is possible but politically fragile.
2008 & 2020

Crisis Responses and Large-Scale Money Creation

Financial crisis and pandemic trigger massive deficits and Federal Reserve asset purchases. Debt and the money supply rise sharply.

When the economy got very sick in 2008 and again in 2020, the government borrowed a huge amount of money and the central bank created a lot of new money to help. The total debt got much bigger.
The 2008 Global Financial Crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic produced the largest peacetime deficits in modern U.S. history. The Fed engaged in large-scale asset purchases (quantitative easing), expanding its balance sheet dramatically. Debt held by the public rose from ~$5T (2007) to ~$10T (2012) and then accelerated further after 2020. Key legislation: TARP (2008), CARES Act (2020). Philosophy: in a pure fiat system, the government and central bank can respond to crises with large-scale money and debt creation; the constraint is confidence and inflation rather than gold.
Key legislation: TARP, CARES Act. Result: Debt and Fed balance sheet expand sharply. Demonstrates the crisis-response capacity (and long-term cost) of unconstrained fiat money.
October 2026

Current Picture

Total debt ~$40.3 trillion. Debt held by the public ~$32.4–32.5T. Interest costs ~$1.1–1.25T per year.

The government owes a huge amount of money. Most of it is owed to regular people, banks, and other countries — not just to itself.
Debt held by the public is the economically relevant portion (owned by Fed ~$4.57T, mutual funds, pensions, individuals, foreigners ~$9.25T). Top foreign holders: Japan, UK, China. Intragovernmental holdings (~$7.8T) are mostly trust funds. Dollar reserve status and deep Treasury markets sustain demand. Inflation target + political incentives continue the pressure.
Total: ~$40.3T
Held by public: ~$32.4T (~100% of GDP)
Interest: ~$1.1–1.25T (2nd largest budget item)
Foreign holdings: ~$9.25T
2030s (projections)

Where Current Policy Points

Under current law, debt held by the public is projected to reach ~120% of GDP by 2036. Interest costs continue rising. Social Security trust-fund pressure arrives ~2032.

If nothing changes, the amount the government owes will keep growing faster than the economy, and more money will go just to paying interest.
CBO February 2026 baseline: debt/GDP to 120% by 2036, interest toward $2T. Higher rates accelerate the path. Political difficulty of cutting entitlements or raising broad taxes makes large primary surpluses unlikely without a crisis or major reform. Base case is gradual erosion of fiscal space; tail risk is loss of confidence and faster rate rise.
Key tension: System can continue while dollar demand and real growth hold. Sustainability is not guaranteed indefinitely under unchanged policy.
How can the USA sustain such a high national debt?

The debt is sustainable while investors remain willing to lend to the U.S. government at acceptable rates. That willingness rests on the dollar’s reserve-currency role, the depth of Treasury markets, the U.S. tax base, and the Federal Reserve’s ability to manage rates. It is not free: rising interest costs crowd out other spending, and confidence can weaken if deficits grow faster than the economy for too long.

Who are we in debt to? What does “debt held by the public” mean?

Debt held by the public is the portion owned by outside investors — individuals, mutual funds, pension funds, banks, the Federal Reserve, and foreign governments. It is the economically meaningful measure. Intragovernmental holdings (mostly Social Security and other trust funds) are internal accounting entries. Roughly a quarter of the public debt is held by foreigners (Japan, the UK, China, and others); the rest is domestic.

How do Treasury securities work?

The Treasury borrows by auctioning securities (bills, notes, bonds, and inflation-protected securities). Investors bid; the highest prices (lowest yields) win. The government pays interest and returns principal at maturity. These securities are among the world’s safest and most liquid assets, which helps sustain demand even as the stock of debt grows.

Can the U.S. government really just print more money?

In a pure fiat system the Federal Reserve can create bank reserves and purchase Treasuries. That expands the money supply. It does not create real goods or services. If money grows faster than the economy’s productive capacity, the result is inflation. Creating money is a policy choice with consequences, not a free lunch.

How does inflation work in this context?

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. The current regime targets roughly 2% inflation. That means cash and fixed claims slowly lose purchasing power. The target gives the central bank room to lower real rates in a downturn, but it also means savers must put money into real assets or accept gradual erosion.

Why is inflation treated as the baseline? Is there a model where the dollar gains value?

Under a commodity standard the money supply was constrained by the metal, so prices could fall as well as rise. The post-1971 pure fiat system with an explicit inflation target makes mild inflation the policy baseline. A model in which the dollar gains value over time would require an external constraint — a commodity link, a fixed supply rule, or a hard fiscal limit — that prevents continuous expansion of money relative to real output.
